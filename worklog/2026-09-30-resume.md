## The finding that sets the next step

The multi-second holds the lock log recorded (getrawmempool 3,467 ms,
getmempoolinfo 2,885 ms, the facade's getrawtransaction 2,045 ms) were
**not those handlers' CPU**: measured on production they cost 10–13 ms and
550–640 ms. They were the handlers waiting on the **pool lock** (`mpl()`,
the mutex the worker process owns), held by the worker for seconds. Each
of those handlers consults the mempool under that lock. The 90-second
stalls of the whole surface, each within two seconds of a new block, have
the same shape one size up: at a block the worker removes thousands of
transactions from the pool and recomputes clusters, plausibly under the
pool lock for a long time.

1. **DONE (#359).** **Instrument the worker's pool mutex** exactly as #348 did for the
   execution lock: time every take and release in the worker (and in the
   RPC process's `mpl()`), log a hold or a wait over a threshold with the
   site's name (`removeForBlock`, the cluster recompute, the journal write,
   the compaction pass, `tx_accept`), keep the last few lines for a test.
   Deploy, wait for the next block-time stall, read the name. This is the
   likely 90-second holder; do not build a fix before the line names it.
2. **DONE for the mempool consult (#359); the block lane is not done.**
   **`getrawtransaction` off the execution lock.** It is the facade's
   second most-dispatched call and it consults the mempool under the pool
   lock first, so it stalls the surface whenever the worker holds that
   lock. Either a block lane with a private block buffer (it and `getblock`
   share `g_blockbuf` and the undo arrays, `rpc_chain.c`), or at least the
   mempool consult done outside the execution lock the way #349 did for the
   worker waits. `getblock`, `getblockstats`, `gettxout` and the facade's
   own `esp_lock` dispatch remain on the exclusive lock too.
3. **Finer download requests inside Core's 1,024-block window.** Run 31
   lost 11.5% to run 30 because ten workers fetching 40-block chunks
   starve the apply in a short runway (its download lag alternated between
   the full window and zero); Core keeps 16 blocks in flight per peer and
   refills one at a time. Shape the worker's requests likewise (a rolling
   in-flight set, or smaller chunks with more of them in flight), keep the
   window at Core's 1,024, and run 32 to see whether the 11.5% comes back.
   The IBD benchmark harness and its protocol are `validation/fresh_ibd_run.sh`
   with `PARITY=1` (the default); the checklist is `docs/devlog/BENCHMARKS.md`,
   "The even comparison".
4. **A download-only pair**, after item 3: bmc's parallel downloader writing
   the archive without the apply, against Core with `-debug=net` so its
   per-block receipt times can be read, same link, same day. Core has no
   download-only mode; its number comes from the net log. This is the only
   way to compare download performance in isolation, since on this link
   both nodes are apply-bound from ~350,000 on.
